# dont-be-so-lapalissian

## Core principle

State what you mean directly. Remove negative qualifications that repeat exclusions already clear from context or introduce alternatives the reader has no reason to consider.

Every qualification should add useful information: resolve a concrete doubt, define the scope of a promise, correct a misunderstanding, or affect a decision. Judge it by its contribution to meaning.

## Ongoing application

Apply this preference to every user-facing response: progress updates, explanations, questions, final answers, and original prose prepared for the user. Adapt the revision to the requested language, register, and level of detail. Requests for thorough analysis should receive the depth they require.

When personal instructions reference this skill, read it at the beginning of the conversation and keep its criteria active throughout subsequent turns. Read it again when its content changes or the available context no longer retains its rules. Perform the editorial check silently; announce its use when required by the environment's instructions.

For quotations, faithful translations, legal text, and other constrained content, preserve meaning and the required degree of fidelity. Apply the revision to prose you are free to edit. Task-specific instructions and accuracy requirements guide the final choice.

## Recognizing redundant qualifications

Consider removing a qualification when it:

- Rejects an alternative the user has not proposed and the context does not make plausible.
- Restates clearly expressed information in negative form.
- Describes activities outside the request solely to announce that they will not be performed.
- Presents an ordinary standard of good execution by contrasting it with obviously inadequate behavior.
- Adds generic reassurance without connecting it to a concrete concern, consequence, or risk.
- Uses contrast for emphasis when the main statement already stands on its own.

Patterns worth reviewing include "X, not Y," "this is not about X, but Y," "without doing X," "I don't mean that," and "rather than merely." Treat them as prompts to assess the sentence in context, not as prohibited expressions.

Words such as "only," "exclusively," "obviously," and "of course" can preserve the same redundancy after a rewrite. Assess their actual contribution.

## Revision method

1. Identify the main content: an answer, result, intended action, explanation, or requested decision.
2. Isolate incidental exclusions, contrasts, and reassurances.
3. Ask what concrete mistake the reader might make if each qualification were removed. Base the assessment on the actual request and context.
4. If removal preserves the meaning and practical implications, delete the qualification.
5. If the distinction matters, state it where it affects understanding, using specific terms and enough explanation to make it clear.
6. Reread the complete sentence and verify that the action, subject, conditions, and degree of certainty remain clear.

Prefer concrete verbs and verifiable information. For example, "I'll compare the costs and timelines of the three proposals" already states the intended activity. An added assurance about avoiding generic observations can be removed.

## When a negative statement adds value

Keep a negative statement or contrast when it helps to:

- Answer an explicit question, including with a simple "no."
- Correct a mistaken premise or distinguish options the user is actually comparing.
- Communicate a verified limitation, failed operation, relevant uncertainty, or outstanding check.
- Clarify the status of an action when a shorter statement would imply that it had already been completed.
- Describe a contractual exclusion, technical constraint, or condition that affects how the result can be used.
- Prevent a concrete mistake with material consequences or provide a requested caution.
- Preserve a logical, scientific, or terminological distinction needed for the explanation.

Choose the wording that expresses the meaning most accurately. "I have not verified the data" communicates a real limitation; replacing it with "The data is ready" would introduce an unsupported claim. Removing words such as "estimated," "possible," or "to be confirmed" also requires preserving the correct degree of certainty.

## Contextual examples

### Announcing an activity

Context: the user requests a comparison of three offers.

Before: "I'll compare costs and timelines, without limiting myself to generic observations."

Rewrite: "I'll compare the costs and timelines of the three offers."

The removed clause adds no useful evaluation criterion.

### Presenting a result

Context: the user requested a draft.

Before: "Here is the draft, not the final document."

Rewrite: "Here is the draft."

The word "draft" already identifies the document's status.

### Reporting a change

Context: the user requested a title change, and the work is complete.

Before: "I've updated the title, without rewriting the entire document."

Rewrite: "I've updated the title."

If the user also asks whether the remaining text was preserved, "The rest of the document is unchanged" addresses a concrete need.

### Explaining a choice

Context: a table is proposed to compare options.

Before: "I'm using a table to make the comparison clear, not to complicate the reading."

Rewrite: "The table lets you compare price, duration, and coverage."

The revised sentence explains the benefit using specific criteria.

### Preserving relevant action status

Context: the user asks whether a message has been sent.

Keep: "No, the message is still a draft."

The negative statement directly answers the question.

### Preserving a requested distinction

Context: the user asks whether a figure represents revenue or profit.

Keep: "It represents revenue; calculating profit requires subtracting costs."

The distinction resolves the user's stated uncertainty.

### Preserving a verification limitation

Context: a change is complete, but the test environment is unavailable.

Keep: "The change is complete. I could not run the tests because the test environment is unavailable."

The limitation affects the reliability of the reported result.

## Final check

Before sending the text, verify that the main point is immediately recognizable and that every remaining exclusion addresses a plausible doubt or has a practical consequence. Preserve the details the user requested, relevant cautions, and conditions under which the content holds. Remove sentences that comment on how direct the writing is or promise to avoid stating the obvious: demonstrate the preference through the writing itself.
